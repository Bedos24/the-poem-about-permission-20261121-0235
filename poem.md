# May I

At six, I stood where our yard gave out
and asked my mother, standing in the door,
if I might cross the road to Michael's house —
she weighed me once, then nodded. I was rich.

My father kept his tools in a locked shed —
the saw, the awl, the plane with its iron mouth.
"You may," he said, "when you can name the teeth,
and tell me which way every blade can bite."

Years on, a nurse in a blue corridor
paused with her hand above my father's chest.
"May I?" she asked the room, and waited there
until he nodded, and the touch was kind.

I learned the rest the night you stood with me
outside my door, your hand not yet a knock,
and asked — not took — and in that asking gave
the only gift the taking never could.

Once, refused, I cursed a friend for weeks:
he would not lend his father's boat to me.
The winter the lake kept two boys under,
I blessed the door that would not open then.

Now children stand at the edge of my yard
and ask me, framed in my lit doorway, May I —
and I feel the whole weight of the small word yes,
how it must be given slowly, like a key.
